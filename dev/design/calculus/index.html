    Design notes: calculus#

    Piecewise functions#

    • SymJ.piecewise(c₁ => v₁, …) is an expression, not a Cases: a function node piecewise(c₁, v₁, c₂, v₂, …) whose conditions are nodes too, Lt(a, b) and its kin and And(…). A relation between constants evaluates to 1 or 0, so substitution picks the piece; a condition linear in one symbol is solved for it (1 - x < 0 as x > 1). As built, false pieces are dropped, a true one ends the list, and a piece with the value of the true one after it goes; with none left the value is NaN.

    • Taken piece by piece: diff; N and lambdify evaluate only the piece taken (an if chain), so piecewise(x < 0 => log(-x), true => sqrt(x)) is real throughout; refine decides conditions, heaviside, sign and abs under assumptions; limit replaces each piecewise, heaviside and dirac by its value on the side approached (the sign of a condition from the limit of g, or of g/t^j when that is 0), the two sides compared for a two-sided limit.

    • heaviside(x, h0 = 1/2), dirac(x, k = 0): diff(heaviside) = dirac, diff(sign(x)) = 2 dirac(x). piecewise(ex) writes heaviside, sign, abs, max, min as pieces (real arguments); piecewise(cs) turns cases into one.

    • Integration (in the RischIntegrate extension, a method of integrate for Sym): a dirac(m x + c, k) term by ∫ g δ⁽ᵏ⁾(x - a) = (-1)^k g⁽ᵏ⁾(a) heaviside(x - a); the rest between consecutive breakpoints (sorted real numbers, or a single one with parameters), the integrand read at a point inside each interval and the constants chosen for continuity. abs, sign, max and min, which RischIntegrate does not take, are rewritten as pieces when it fails on them.

    • Folding (SymJ.piecewise_fold(e, x)): heaviside, sign, abs, max, min rewritten as pieces, the breakpoints (roots of the conditions) found and ordered, as numbers or by the assumptions, the parameters taken to be real; an order they do not decide, and a condition they do not decide at a point, are split into cases (the machinery of Cases: a < b, a = b, a > b, the equal points merged), then the value on each open interval (at a point inside) and at each breakpoint (exactly), each condition decided by the sign of its difference under the assumptions. Pieces are merged: an interval into the piece before it when the formulas agree, a breakpoint when the formula before it gives its value there (else it starts a piece that the next interval joins when that formula gives the value); each condition is by the piece's right end (x < b, x ≤ b, true). Without x, the one symbol of the conditions. simplify tries it for two or more piecewise functions, kept when smaller: ∫₀¹ |x - a| dx, a difference of two piecewise functions from the integrator, becomes three pieces in a.

    • Printing: piecewise(x ≪ 0 => -x, true => x); in LaTeX a cases environment, heaviside and dirac as θ and δ.

    Summation#

    • SymJ.summation(f, k, a, b), a chain of methods as in SymPy: linearity; direct addition for a short numeric range; an antidifference F (F(k+1) - F(k) = f(k)) giving F(b+1) - F(a), by Bernoulli polynomials for polynomials and Gosper's algorithm for hypergeometric terms (the term ratio from polynomials, r^k, factorials, binomials, gamma; the Gosper form through resultant and gcd; the polynomial x(k) from its degree bound and a linear system); partial fractions for rational functions without one (harmonic numbers, polygamma, exact ζ(2n) by Bernoulli numbers); known sums (the binomial theorem; ₀F₀, ₁F₀, ₀F₁ hypergeometric series: exp, cos, sin, cosh, sinh).

    • Zeilberger's algorithm for a definite sum whose upper bound involves n and whose summand has no antidifference: creative telescoping, Gosper's equation with the coefficients c_j(n) of a recurrence Σ_j c_j(n) S(n+j) = 0 as further unknowns of the same linear system. A first-order recurrence is solved as a product (factorials, half-integer shifts through (2n)!/(4^n n!), presented as binomials); a recurrence of order 2 or 3 by its hypergeometric solutions (Hyper, below), combined to match the sum's first values. Each closed form is checked against direct sums for several n (and integer values of other parameters); a failed check leaves the sum unevaluated. With the summand's own bounds the recurrence is homogeneous, and that is tried first. Otherwise (an upper bound s n + t, s a positive integer, a lower one free of n) the telescoper Σ_j c_j F(n+j, k) = G(n, k+1) - G(n, k), G = R F with R from Gosper's solution, summed over k = a..b(n), gives the right side G(n, b+1) - G(n, a) + Σ_j c_j Σ_{k=b(n)+1}^{b(n+j)} F(n+j, k); G(n, b+1) is taken through the ratio F(n, b+1)/F(n, b) as a rational function, so a zero of F there meets a pole of R. The inhomogeneous recurrence is made homogeneous as in rsolve (below) and solved by Hyper: Σ_{k=0}^{n-1} binomial(n, k) = 2^n - 1, Σ_{k=0}^{n} binomial(n, k)/(k + 1) = (2^{n+1} - 1)/(n + 1), Σ_{k=0}^{n} binomial(2n, k) = (4^n + binomial(2n, n))/2.

    • Right sides. rsolve takes Σ_i p_i y(n+i) = g with g a sum of hypergeometric terms: grouped into classes (a rational ratio between members), each class annihilated in turn by L ↦ ρ_den(n) L(n+1) - ρ_num(n) L(n), which maps every other class c to c (ρ_den ρ_c - ρ_num) (rational multiples, so still one term each), one order higher per class, then Hyper on the homogeneous recurrence. Powers with shifted exponents are merged in the answer (8·2^(n - 3) as 2^n).

    • Hyper (Petkovšek), for Σ_i p_i(n) y(n+i) = 0: a hypergeometric solution has y(n+1)/y(n) = Z A(n)/B(n) C(n+1)/C(n) with A a monic factor of p_0(n), B of p_d(n-d+1); for each pair, Z a nonzero root of the leading coefficients of P_i = p_i Π_{j<i} A(n+j) Π_{j≥i} B(n+j), and C a polynomial solution of Σ Z^i P_i C(n+i) = 0, its degree bounded by the indicial polynomial of the recurrence written in differences (E = 1 + Δ), its coefficients from a nullspace. When that finds too few solutions, it is run again with the coefficients' irreducible factors of higher degree split over their roots (radicals), so A = n - sqrt(2) can divide n^2 - 2; the values used to fit the solution are simplified first, as they have Γ at algebraic shifts. SymJ.rsolve(eq, y(n), inits...) reads the recurrence, raises a hypergeometric right side g to a homogeneous recurrence one order higher (L(y)(n+1) - ρ(n) L(y)(n) = 0, ρ = g(n+1)/g(n)), fits the solutions to the initial values (or to y(0), …, y(d-1) themselves) and checks the result against values from the recurrence.

    • d'Alembertian solutions (Abramov–Petkovšek), when Hyper's solutions do not span: reduction of order. A hypergeometric solution h of the homogeneous recurrence, with y = h z and z(n+i) = z(n) + Σ_{j<i} u(n+j) (u = Δz), gives Σ_j s_j u(n+j) = g/h, s_j = Σ_{i>j} p_i h(n+i)/h(n), one order lower (the coefficient of z(n) is L(h)/h = 0). At order 0, u = g/s_0, so the right side need not be hypergeometric. Each level adds a solution h Σ_{m=n0}^{n-1} u(m); n0 is past the integer zeros and poles of every level's ratios and leading coefficient. The constants are fitted with the sums unevaluated (added up at numbers, so polygamma need not evaluate), then the sums are summed, innermost first, and the answer is checked in the unevaluated form: y(n+1) - y(n) = 1/(n+1) gives harmonic(n), y(n+1) = (n+1) y(n) + 1 gives n! (1 + Σ 1/(k+1)!). Zeilberger's recurrences go the same way when Hyper falls short: Σ_{k=1}^{n} (-1)^(k+1) binomial(n, k)/k = harmonic(n). A level without a hypergeometric solution ends it, so the solution is not d'Alembertian.

    • Liouvillian solutions (Hendriks–Singer: y(n+m)/y(n) rational for some m ≤ d), by sections. y(n+t) is reduced by the recurrence to the basis y(n), …, y(n+d-1) plus a term free of y; the first linear dependency among y(n), y(n+m), …, y(n+km) over the rational functions (a nullspace) gives Σ_j c_j(n) y(n+jm) = r(n), and n = mN + i a recurrence for z_i(N) = y(mN + i). Each section is solved from its own values (Hyper, else d'Alembertian), and the answer is piecewise(mod(n, m) ~ 0 => z_0(n/m), …): y(n+2) = (n+1) y(n) gives the double factorial, and Zeilberger's (n+2) S(n+2) + 4(n+1) S(n) = 0 gives Σ (-1)^k binomial(n, k)^2 = (-1)^(n/2) binomial(n, n/2) for even n, 0 for odd. Products with Γ(N + j/m) for every j = 1..m-1 are combined by Gauss's multiplication theorem, Π_j Γ(z + j/m) = (2π)^((m-1)/2) m^(1/2-mz) Γ(mz)/Γ(z), and b^(cN) is taken as (b^c)^N before powers are merged, so Dixon's Σ (-1)^k binomial(n, k)^3 is (-1)^(n/2) (3n/2)!/((n/2)!)^3. The Franel recurrence of Σ binomial(n, k)^3 has no Liouvillian solution, and that sum stays unevaluated.

    • The recurrence as the answer. When none of these apply, rsolve returns DifferenceRoot(x, k, y, d, m0, g, p_0, …, p_d, v_0, …, v_{d-1}) (as Mathematica's DifferenceRoot): Σ p_i(k) y(k+i) = g(k) with y(m0 + j) = v_j, at x. The coefficients are in a bound symbol k, so substituting for x leaves them alone; it prints as the recurrence in x's symbol, highest shift first with factored coefficients, and at an integer x its value is the recurrence unrolled (exact, up to 100 000 steps). There is no arithmetic on these (sums and products of holonomic sequences, asymptotics); summation keeps Sum, which is clearer for a sum.

    • Harmonic numbers, Σ_{k=a}^{b} g(k) H(k + c) with c an integer and g with an antidifference G, by parts: G(b+1) H(b+c+1) - G(a) H(a+c) - Σ G(k+1)/(k+c+1). A polynomial G is shifted by a constant to vanish at -c, so the last sum is of a polynomial; the answer is written with H(b+c+1) or H(b+c), whichever is smaller: Σ_{k=1}^{n} k H(k) = n(n+1) H(n)/2 - n(n-1)/4. The same holds for the generalized H(n, m) = Σ_{j=1}^{n} 1/j^m (SymJ.harmonic(n, m)), with ΔH(k + c, m) = 1/(k+c+1)^m; integer-shifted Σ 1/(k + α)^m are themselves H(·, m) (polygamma for other shifts). A rational g without an antidifference is split into partial fractions: the polynomial part by parts, and 1/(k + α)^m with the order of H by Σ_{j=1}^{q} H(j, m)/j^m = (H(q, m)^2 + H(q, 2m))/2 (the pairs i ≤ j), after moving H(k + c, m) to H(k + α, m) by finitely many terms: Σ_{k=1}^{n} H(k)/k = (H(n)^2 + H(n, 2))/2. Other powers are Euler sums (Σ H(k)/k^2), with no closed form for finite n, and stay unevaluated, as does a sum whose last part does not close (Σ 2^k H(k)).

    • Infinite sums are limits of the antidifference (limit). A polynomial times r^k converges exactly when -1 < r < 1, decided by cases on a parameter.

    • Otherwise the sum stays unevaluated, Sum(f, k, a, b), which adds up once its bounds become numbers. The name is summation, not a method of Base.sum, since the call is different (an index and bounds, not an iterable).

    Differential equations#

    • SymJ.dsolve(eq, y(x), ics...) reads the equation as F(x, Y₀, …, Yₙ) = 0, the derivatives of y(x) replaced by placeholder symbols, and tries the methods in turn: a missing y (solve for v = y⁽ʲ⁾, then integrate), linear (F affine in the Yₖ: the integrating factor at first order; constant coefficients by the characteristic roots, with x^j for a multiple root and e^(αx) cos βx, e^(αx) sin βx for a conjugate pair; Cauchy–Euler by x = eᵗ, which turns xᵏ y⁽ᵏ⁾ into θ(θ - 1)…(θ - k + 1) y), then first order y' = f(x, y): separable (f factors as g(x)h(y)), Bernoulli, exact (with an integrating factor in x or y alone), homogeneous (f(x, vx) free of x).

    • Riccati equations, y' = q₀ + q₁ y + q₂ y², the last first-order method: y = -u'/(q₂ u) gives u'' - (q₁ + q₂'/q₂) u' + q₀ q₂ u = 0, solved when it has constant coefficients or is Cauchy–Euler, and u = C u₁ + u₂ gives y with one constant. Otherwise a particular solution y₁ = c xᵏ (|k| ≤ 2) or c e^(kx) (|k| ≤ 2), with c constant, and y = y₁ + 1/v, v from a linear equation. A first-order method whose answer has an unevaluated integral is kept only if no later one does better: y' = y² + 4 is separable, but without RischIntegrate the integral is left undone, while as a Riccati equation it has a closed form in sin and cos.

    • Autonomous equations, second order without x: with p = y' a function of s = y, y'' = p dp/ds, a first-order equation for p(s) solved by the methods above; then ∫ ds/p(s) = x + C₂, solved for y when that gives one solution and implicit otherwise. When p has several branches (p = ±sqrt(q(s)), as for y'' = -sin y) each gives a solution, and dsolve returns a vector. With initial conditions y(a) = v, y'(a) = w, C₁ is fixed first from the relation between p and s at (v, w), the branches through (v, w) kept, and only then ∫ ds/p taken, which is often elementary when the general one is not (y'' = 2y³, y(0) = 1, y'(0) = 1: C₁ = 0, y = 1/(1 - x)); without a closed form it is the definite ∫ᵥʸ ds/p = x - a, an Integral(f, y, v, y(x)) node printed Integral(f, (y, v, y(x))) as integrate takes a definite integral; y is bound there (for subs and free_symbols), and diff uses the fundamental theorem. sqrt(exp(2y)) is exp(y), y being real. An implicit solution of any order is checked, and its constants fixed by initial conditions at one point, through implicit differentiation: y' = -φₓ/φ_y and each higher derivative by d/dx + y' d/dy. When the explicit form gives a nonlinear system for the constants (y = e^(C₁(C₂ + x))), the implicit one is used.

    • Particular solutions. Undetermined coefficients when every term of the right side is c xᵐ e^(αx) cos(βx) (or sin); the trial form is multiplied by xˢ, s the multiplicity of α + iβ as a root, and the coefficients come from a linear system. Otherwise variation of parameters, with the Wronskian.

    • Solutions are explicit y = S or implicit φ(x, y) = C; an implicit one is solved for y when that gives one solution (with an initial condition, the branch through the point). Each is checked by substitution (implicitly, y' = -φₓ/φ_y), symbolically and else numerically at a few points. Constants are absorbed where one appears once: k C is C, and e^C in an exponent is C.

    • Derivatives as values. Derivative(f, v) binds v: substituting a value gives the derivative at a point, Derivative(f, v, a), and substituting into f evaluates it (Derivative(y(x), x) with y(x) => sin(x) is cos(x)). y' on a SymFunction builds these, so initial conditions read y'(0) => 1, and a derivative at a point prints the same way (y''(0), not Derivative(Derivative(y(x), x), x, 0)).

    • Series solutions (series = n, about x0). At an ordinary point, the equation solved for y⁽ᵏ⁾ = f: the first k Taylor coefficients are constants (or initial values), and each next cₘ is the coefficient of tᵐ⁻ᵏ in the series of f(x₀ + t, y, …) with the coefficients so far, which is all it depends on. This is the series code doing the work, so nonlinear equations need nothing more. A pole or a fractional power in that series marks a singular point. There, for a second-order linear homogeneous equation with t p(t), t² q(t) analytic, Frobenius: the indicial equation, the recurrence for aₘ(r) with r a symbol (rational functions, cancelled), and for the second solution ∂/∂r of tʳ Σ aₘ(r) tᵐ (equal roots) or of (r - r₂) tʳ Σ aₘ(r) tᵐ (roots an integer apart) at r₂, which is where the log t term comes from. Each series is checked: its residual is O(tⁿ⁻ᵏ) (Frobenius: over a₂/t², O(t^(r₂ + n))).

    • Laplace transforms (src/laplace.jl). Forward, term by term: dirac(t - a) g is e^(-as) g(a) and heaviside(t - a) g is e^(-as) L{g(t + a)}; anything else has its sin, cos, sinh, cosh written as exponentials, so each term is c tⁿ e^(αt) (α possibly complex), with transform c n!/(s - α)ⁿ⁺¹; the sum is cancelled, which makes it real again. Inverse: the terms grouped by their e^(-cs) factor (a heaviside(t - c) shift each), each group split by apart into N/qᵏ with q irreducible, and inverted by the residues of N e^(st)/qᵏ at the roots of q, then made real as for systems. A quadratic's roots are α ± iβ, β = sqrt(c/a - α²) with the parameters taken to be positive (so s² + w² gives sin(wt)/w, not exponentials of sqrt(-w²)); either square root gives the same function, a conjugate pair being even in β. dsolve uses them for a linear equation (or system) with constant coefficients when asked (method = :laplace) or when the right side has heaviside or dirac, where the other methods leave integrals undone: Y(s) = (L{g} + Σₖ aₖ Σⱼ sᵏ⁻¹⁻ʲ y⁽ʲ⁾(a)) / Σₖ aₖ sᵏ, with t shifted to the point a of the initial conditions; for a system Z(s) = (sI - M)⁻¹ (Z(a) + L{F}).

    • Systems. SymJ.dsolve([eq₁, …], [y₁(t), …], ics...), linear with constant coefficients. The highest derivative of each unknown is solved for (the matrix of their coefficients must be invertible: the system in normal form), and the system made first order, Z' = M Z + F(t), with Z the unknowns and their lower derivatives. With M = P J P⁻¹ (Jordan form), the columns of P exp(J t) are solutions; a conjugate pair of complex columns is replaced by its real and imaginary parts (i rewritten out of denominators first, and e^((a + ib)t) as e^(at)(cos bt + i sin bt)), giving a real fundamental matrix Φ. The general solution is Φ C, so a defective M gives t^k e^(λt) terms. A particular solution is P exp(J t) ∫ exp(-J t) P⁻¹ F dt when the integrand, with sin, cos, sinh, cosh as exponentials, is a sum of t^k e^(at) (integrated in closed form, then made real); otherwise Φ ∫ Φ⁻¹ F dt through the integrator. Parameters in M are taken to be real.

    • Integrals go through an integrator hook that the RischIntegrate extension sets; without it a small integrator does polynomials, powers of a linear function, and exp, sin, cos, sinh, cosh of linear arguments, and anything else is an unevaluated Integral(f, x) whose derivative is f.
