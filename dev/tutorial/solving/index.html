    Solving#

    Solving equations: roots of polynomials, equations and inequalities in one unknown, and systems.

    SymJ provides solvers for different types of equations, the most general being solve, described here along with a few other methods. In the Calculus section we also introduce rsolve and dsolve.

    Equations are specified with ~ (as is common in Julia) or Eq. This infix operator binds more loosely than everything else, so no parentheses are typically needed. Equations are held internally as a Relation. The left- and right-hand sides of a relation are returned by lhs and rhs respectively.

    Roots of polynomials#

    We will see more uses later, but solve finds the roots of a polynomial. Degrees up to four are solved exactly, with real roots of cubics given in trigonometric form (no complex numbers needed):

    p = expand((x^2 + x)^3 - 2)
solve(p ~ 0, x)
    \[\left[ - \frac{\sqrt{4 \cdot 2^{\frac{1}{3}} + 1}}{2} - \frac{1}{2}, \; \frac{\sqrt{4 \cdot 2^{\frac{1}{3}} + 1}}{2} - \frac{1}{2}, \; - \frac{\sqrt{4 \cdot \left( -1 \right)^{\frac{2}{3}} \cdot 2^{\frac{1}{3}} + 1}}{2} - \frac{1}{2}, \; - \frac{\sqrt{-4 \cdot \left( -1 \right)^{\frac{1}{3}} \cdot 2^{\frac{1}{3}} + 1}}{2} - \frac{1}{2}, \; \frac{\sqrt{-4 \cdot \left( -1 \right)^{\frac{1}{3}} \cdot 2^{\frac{1}{3}} + 1}}{2} - \frac{1}{2}, \; \frac{\sqrt{4 \cdot \left( -1 \right)^{\frac{2}{3}} \cdot 2^{\frac{1}{3}} + 1}}{2} - \frac{1}{2} \right]\] 
    solve(x^3 - 3x + 1 ~ 0, x)
    \[\left[ 2 \cdot \cos\left( \frac{10 \cdot \pi}{9} \right), \; 2 \cdot \cos\left( \frac{4 \cdot \pi}{9} \right), \; 2 \cdot \cos\left( \frac{2 \cdot \pi}{9} \right) \right]\] 
    solve(x^4 - 10x^2 + 1 ~ 0, x)
    \[\left[ - \sqrt{2 \cdot \sqrt{6} + 5}, \; - \sqrt{-2 \cdot \sqrt{6} + 5}, \; \sqrt{-2 \cdot \sqrt{6} + 5}, \; \sqrt{2 \cdot \sqrt{6} + 5} \right]\]

    (Neither of the last two examples can be factored with factor, which provides factorization into irreducibles over the rationals.)

    From degree five, it is possible there are no roots in radicals. Those of special forms, such as x^5 ~ 2, are still solved exactly by solve.

    When exact roots aren't identifiable, SymJ.fsolve finds the roots numerically, the real ones by default (with domain = ℂ all of them):

    (
SymJ.fsolve(x^5 - x + 1 ~ 0, x),
SymJ.fsolve(x^5 - x + 1 ~ 0, x; domain = ℂ)
)
    \[\left( \left[ -1.1673039782614187 \right], \; \left[ -1.1673039782614187, \; -1.0839541013177107 \cdot i - 0.18123244446987538, \; 1.0839541013177107 \cdot i - 0.18123244446987538, \; -0.35247154603172626 \cdot i + 0.7648844336005848, \; 0.35247154603172626 \cdot i + 0.7648844336005848 \right] \right)\]

    The real roots of a polynomial with rational coefficients can be found numerically, with certainty, when the closed form is unwieldy. SymJ.real_roots gives them correctly rounded, each repeated by its multiplicity, and SymJ.root_intervals exact isolating intervals:

    (
SymJ.real_roots(x^3 - 2x - 5, x),
SymJ.real_roots((x - 1)^2 * (x^2 - 2), x)
)
    ([2.0945514815423265], [-1.4142135623730951, 1.0, 1.0, 1.4142135623730951])
    SymJ.root_intervals(x^2 - 2, x; width = 1 // 100)
    2-element Vector{Tuple{Rational{BigInt}, Rational{BigInt}, Int64}}:
 (-91//64, -181//128, 1)
 (181//128, 91//64, 1)

    Solving equations#

    Non-polynomial equations can also be solved.

    solve(a*sin(x) + b ~ 0, x)
    \[\left[ - \arcsin\left( \frac{b}{a} \right), \; \arcsin\left( \frac{b}{a} \right) + \pi \right]\]

    Answers to solve(eqn, var) are a vector of Sym values, empty when there are no identified solutions, or Cases when the answer depends on the values of the parameters.

    Equations in which x appears through one function are solved by solving for that function first:

    solve(exp(2x) - 3exp(x) + 2 ~ 0, x)
    \[\left[ 0, \; \log\left( 2 \right) \right]\] 
    solve(sqrt(x + 1) ~ x - 1, x)
    \[\left[ 3 \right]\]

    Several logarithms of the unknown are combined; roots where a logarithm would be complex (here x = -2) are dropped:

    solve(log(x) + log(x - 1) ~ log(Sym(6)), x)
    \[\left[ 3 \right]\]

    A product is zero where one of its factors is, so a product (or a sum with a common factor) is solved factor by factor. A factor that is never zero, such as (a/x)^x, gives no roots, and a root where another factor is undefined is dropped (x*log(x) none at 0).

    solve(x * log(x) ~ 0, x)
    \[\left[ 1 \right]\]

    Some equations need the Lambert W function, from the LambertW package:

    solve(x * exp(x) ~ 1, x)
    \[\left[ \mathrm{lambertw}\left( 1 \right) \right]\]

    Generic values#

    Symbols other than the unknown are allowed.

    Such symbols, or parameters, take generic values: an expression in them alone is taken to be nonzero, so the quadratic formula comes out as it is usually written:

    solve(a * x^2 + b * x + c ~ 0, x)
    \[\left[ \frac{ - b - \sqrt{-4 \cdot a \cdot c + b^{2}}}{2 \cdot a}, \; \frac{ - b + \sqrt{-4 \cdot a \cdot c + b^{2}}}{2 \cdot a} \right]\]

    An assumption that makes a coefficient vanish gives that case:

    solve(a * x^2 + b * x + c ~ 0, x; assume = (a ~ 0))
    \[\left[ - \frac{c}{b} \right]\]

    A symbolic exponent gives the principal root only. x^n = 1 has n roots for a positive integer n, but their number depends on n, so with n symbolic the answer is the principal one:

    @syms n
solve(x^n ~ 1, x; assume = (n in Integers, n ≫ 0))
    \[\left[ 1 \right]\]

    With a number for n, all of them are identified:

    solve(x^3 ~ 1, x)
    \[\left[ 1, \; \frac{ - i \cdot \sqrt{3}}{2} - \frac{1}{2}, \; \frac{i \cdot \sqrt{3}}{2} - \frac{1}{2} \right]\]

    Inequalities#

    < and its relatives compare numbers and return true or false, so inequalities use other operators: ≪ (typed \ll), ≦ (\leqq), ≫ (\gg), ≧ (\geqq) and ≶ (\lessgtr).

    The solution to an inequality is a union of intervals:

    solve(x^2 - 1 ≫ 0, x)
    \[\left( -\infty, -1 \right) \cup \left( 1, \infty \right)\] 
    solve((x - 1) / (x + 2) ≧ 0, x)
    \[\left( -\infty, -2 \right) \cup \left[ 1, \infty \right)\]

    With parameters, which an order makes real, the set comes by cases on them:

    solve(x^2 ≦ a, x)
    \[\begin{cases} \emptyset & a < 0 \\ \left\{ 0 \right\} & a = 0 \\ \left[ - \sqrt{a}, \sqrt{a} \right] & a > 0 \end{cases}\]

    More on assumptions#

    Symbols are complex numbers unless a fact says otherwise. Facts are relations, such as x > 0, or membership in one of Integers ⊂ Rationals ⊂ Reals ⊂ Complexes (also written ℤ ⊂ ℚ ⊂ ℝ ⊂ ℂ), such as n in Integers. Facts are passed to the assume keyword, and only the solutions they allow are kept.

    The @assume macro lets facts be written with Julia's familiar Boolean operators, including &&, ||, ! and chained comparisons.

    solve(x^2 ~ 4, x; assume = @assume(x > 0))
    \[\left[ 2 \right]\] 
    solve(x^2 + 1 ~ 0, x; assume = @assume(x in Reals))
    \[\left[ \; \right]\]

    Without the macro, relations use the symbolic operators: x ≫ 0 (or Gt(x, 0)), and n ∈ ℤ for membership.

    solve(x^2 ~ 2, x; assume = (x ∈ ℚ,))
    \[\left[ \; \right]\]

    @assuming gives facts for a block of code (as does the function assuming, with a do block):

    @assuming x > 0 begin
    solve(x^3 ~ 8, x), solve(x^2 - 5x + 6 ~ 0, x; assume = @assume(x > 2))
end
    \[\left( \left[ 2 \right], \; \left[ 3 \right] \right)\]

    The facts are read by the operations that take assume (refine, simplify, solve, limit, …), not by arithmetic: an expression is built the same way inside a block as outside it, so it never depends on the facts in force. (a^4)^(3/4) is not a^3 for every a (it is 1 at a = -1 and at a = im), so 16(a^4)^(3/4)/(9a^2) keeps (a^4)^(3/4) even with a > 0 assumed; simplify or refine in the block applies the facts, and refine = true refines the block's value with them:

    @assuming a > 0 begin
    e = 16(a^4)^(3//4) / (9a^2)
    e, simplify(e)
end
    \[\left( \frac{16 \cdot \left( a^{4} \right)^{\frac{3}{4}}}{9 \cdot a^{2}}, \; \frac{16 \cdot a}{9} \right)\] 
    @assuming refine = true a > 0 begin
    16(a^4)^(3//4) / (9a^2)
end
    \[\frac{16 \cdot a}{9}\]

    Cases#

    When the answer depends on something the facts leave open, the result lists the cases:

    solve(x^2 ~ a, x; assume = @assume(x in Reals, a in Reals))
    \[\begin{cases} \left[ \; \right] & a < 0 \\ \left[ 0 \right] & a = 0 \\ \left[ - \sqrt{a}, \; \sqrt{a} \right] & a > 0 \end{cases}\]

    Without a ∈ ℝ, a may be complex, so the cases are a fact and its negation:

    cs = solve(x^2 ~ a, x; assume = @assume(x > 0))
    \[\begin{cases} \left[ \sqrt{a} \right] & a > 0 \\ \left[ \; \right] & \lnot \left( a > 0 \right) \end{cases}\]

    Calling the cases with a value gives the answer for it:

    cs(a => 4), cs(a => -1)
    \[\left( \left[ 2 \right], \; \left[ \; \right] \right)\]

    Contradictory facts are an error (@assume(x > 1 && x < 0)), and the Assumptions value @assume makes can be reused: A = @assume(x > 0, n in Integers), then assume = A.

    As with polynomials, for an equation solve cannot solve exactly, SymJ.fsolve (with name and functionality mirroring Maple's function) finds its roots numerically: the real ones, on the whole line or an interval, and with domain = ℂ complex ones too.

    (
SymJ.fsolve(cos(x) ~ x, x),
SymJ.fsolve(log(x) + sin(x) ~ 1, x, (0, 4))
)
    \[\left( \left[ 0.7390851332151607 \right], \; \left[ 1.1099563755802675, \; 3.3530284715132392 \right] \right)\]

    External zero finding#

    The packages for numerical root finding also take SymJ expressions as they are, since an expression is callable (lambdify makes a faster function): Roots.find_zero(cos(x) - x, 0.5), or with Newton's method and the exact derivative, find_zero((lambdify(f, x), lambdify(diff(f, x), x)), x0, Roots.Newton()).

    Systems#

    A system of equations gives a vector of solutions, each a Dict from the unknowns to their values. A linear system's answer is a single solution (possibly in terms of free unknowns), yet a vector is returned:

    solve([x + y ~ 3, x - y ~ 1], [x, y])
    \[\left[ \begin{array}{l} \left\{ x = 2, \; y = 1 \right\} \end{array} \right]\]

    With parameters, a generic value is assumed—the pivots are taken to be nonzero (here a + 1; with a ~ -1 assumed the system has no solution):

    eqns, vars = [a * x + y ~ 1, x - y ~ b], [x, y]
solve(eqns, vars)
    \[\left[ \begin{array}{l} \left\{ x = \frac{b + 1}{a + 1}, \; y = \frac{ - a \cdot b + 1}{a + 1} \right\} \end{array} \right]\]

    Polynomial systems are solved through a lex Gröbner basis, which is triangular:

    solve([x^2 + y^2 ~ 1, x ~ y], [x, y])
    \[\left[ \begin{array}{l} \left\{ x = - \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \; y = - \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \right\} \\ \left\{ x = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \; y = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \right\} \end{array} \right]\] 
    solve([x*y ~ 1, x + y ~ 3], [x, y])
    \[\left[ \begin{array}{l} \left\{ x = \frac{\sqrt{5}}{2} + \frac{3}{2}, \; y = - \frac{\sqrt{5}}{2} + \frac{3}{2} \right\} \\ \left\{ x = - \frac{\sqrt{5}}{2} + \frac{3}{2}, \; y = \frac{\sqrt{5}}{2} + \frac{3}{2} \right\} \end{array} \right]\]

    Unknowns that appear only through exponentials, through one function, or through sin and cos of one argument are solved for those first:

    solve([x + y ~ 4, 2^x + 2^y ~ 10], [x, y])
    \[\left[ \begin{array}{l} \left\{ x = 3, \; y = 1 \right\} \\ \left\{ x = 1, \; y = 3 \right\} \end{array} \right]\] 
    solve([sin(x) + cos(y) ~ 1, sin(x) - cos(y) ~ 0], [x, y])
    \[\left[ \begin{array}{l} \left\{ x = \frac{\pi}{6}, \; y = \frac{\pi}{3} \right\} \\ \left\{ x = \frac{\pi}{6}, \; y = \frac{5 \cdot \pi}{3} \right\} \\ \left\{ x = \frac{5 \cdot \pi}{6}, \; y = \frac{\pi}{3} \right\} \\ \left\{ x = \frac{5 \cdot \pi}{6}, \; y = \frac{5 \cdot \pi}{3} \right\} \end{array} \right]\]

    With infinitely many solutions, the unknowns left free map to themselves:

    solve([x^2 + y^2 ~ 1], [x, y])
    \[\left[ \begin{array}{l} \left\{ x = - \sqrt{ - y^{2} + 1}, \; y = y \right\} \\ \left\{ x = \sqrt{ - y^{2} + 1}, \; y = y \right\} \end{array} \right]\]

    A system of linear inequalities gives a description of the region, each unknown between bounds in the ones after it:

    solve([x ≧ 0, y ≧ 0, x + y ≦ 1], [x, y])
    \[y \in \left[ 0, 1 \right], \; x \in \left[ 0, - y + 1 \right]\]
